This problem comes from one of my Shogi Wars games. I originally played White; the sides have been swapped here so that you play Black.

## Answer

**N*85 → G83 → B84**

```board
sfen: l3k2nl/3r2gb1/pPg1pp2p/5sPP1/3p3R1/1spB1Sn2/P2PPP2P/2S6/L2GKG1NL b N2P2p 1
moves: N*8e 7c8c 6f8d
caption: Intermediate position
```

Drop the knight to attack the gold, then offer the bishop for free on 84. It is an extraordinary idea.

**Gx84 → N73+**

```board
sfen: l3k2nl/3r2gb1/pPg1pp2p/5sPP1/3p3R1/1spB1Sn2/P2PPP2P/2S6/L2GKG1NL b N2P2p 1
moves: N*8e 7c8c 6f8d 8c8d 8e7c+
caption: Answer sequence
```

After the gold captures the bishop, the knight promotes and attacks the rook. My original analysis gave the attacking side a slight advantage, around 200 points.

## Why does losing a bishop work?

- The defending gold is drawn away from the king.
- Attacking the rook gains time to continue the attack.
- The promoted knight strongly restricts the left side of the enemy king.
- The pawn advance to 23 with promotion remains another promising continuation.

I was impressed that the attacking side could still be at least equal after giving up an entire bishop.

## What I played

Confused by the position, I chose the straightforward pawn advance to 23 with promotion. That attack did not get a strong enough foothold, and my position quickly deteriorated.

There were two separate difficulties: finding the knight drop and bishop sacrifice, and judging that the resulting position was playable. This was difficult enough that I could accept not finding it in the game.

Still, the knight drop is a recognizable local tactic, so I felt a professional might well find it. Drawing a gold forward with a sacrifice can help an attack keep going; that is an idea I want to remember.
